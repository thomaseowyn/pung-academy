Writing & Resources

Bryan Vegard Suwarno

"If a beginner has to read the sentence twice, the sentence is wrong. Not the beginner."

Pung Academy Resource curation Technical writing

My route into technology started in a library rather than a computer lab. I spent two years convinced I was a words person and nothing else — someone who wrote essays, edited the school newsletter, and quietly assumed that anything involving a terminal belonged to a different sort of student entirely. That assumption lasted until a friend asked me to proofread a tutorial they were writing about loops.

I could not proofread it, because I could not follow it. And the longer I stared at the page, the clearer it became that the problem was not the concept. A loop is not a difficult idea. The problem was that every explanation I could find had been written by someone who had forgotten what it felt like not to know. Terms arrived before their definitions. Examples solved problems I did not have yet. The tutorial was accurate and completely unusable, and being annoyed about that is, in the end, what made me learn to code.

Most beginner material is not too hard. It is written in the wrong order.

What I do at Pung Academy

I read everything before it reaches the shelf. Every resource we recommend has been worked through start to finish by someone on the team, and usually that someone is me. If a video spends eleven minutes on setup before the first line of code, I say so. If a guide is excellent but assumes three chapters of prior knowledge, I write the note that tells you what to read first. The goal is a short shelf you can trust rather than a long list you have to evaluate yourself — because evaluating resources is a skill you do not have yet on day one, and it is unfair to require it.

I also write the connective material: the paragraph at the top of each stage explaining why this topic comes now, and the checkpoint text that helps you judge honestly whether you have understood something or merely recognised it. Those are the pieces nobody notices when they work.

Where I am heading

I want to be a developer advocate, or whatever that role ends up being called — someone who sits between the people building a thing and the people trying to learn it, and refuses to let either side give up on the other. In the shorter term I am working through JavaScript properly, having previously learned it in the scattered way I now spend my time arguing against.
