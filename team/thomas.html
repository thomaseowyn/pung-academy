The long way round

I started late and badly. At seventeen I signed up for an online course that promised I would be employable in twelve weeks, and I believed it completely. Nine weeks in I could produce a working result by pattern-matching against the previous example, and I could not explain a single line of what I had written. When the course ended I did what everyone in that position does: I bought another course, assuming the fault was the teacher.

It took a fairly bleak afternoon and an unfinished project to understand what had actually gone wrong. I had been given content in an order nobody had thought about. Every week was interesting on its own and none of it accumulated. The fix, when I finally found it, was almost insultingly simple: go back to the beginning, spend three unglamorous weeks on the fundamentals I had skipped, and stop starting anything new until I had finished one small thing end to end.

What I do here

I am the person who asks whether the roadmap is working, and who insists on an answer that is not just a feeling. That means paying attention to where people stop — which stage, which lesson, which paragraph — and treating each of those as a design problem rather than a motivation problem. When a lot of learners abandon the same checkpoint, the checkpoint is wrong. When everyone breezes through a stage, it is probably in the wrong place.

Practically, that puts me between the curriculum and the interface. I work with Kevin on where the checkpoints sit, with Bryan on whether an explanation is doing its job, and with Elvin on whether the next step is genuinely obvious on a small screen at eleven at night, which is when a lot of learning actually happens.

Where this goes

I want to work in product analytics for an education company — the kind of role where the metric that matters is not how long someone stayed on the page but whether they came back and finished. Long term, I would like Pung Academy to outlive the four of us being students, and be the thing we point at when someone asks where to start.

One thing I believe

Nobody quits programming because it is hard. They quit because they cannot tell whether they are making progress. Fix the second problem and the first one becomes survivable.
