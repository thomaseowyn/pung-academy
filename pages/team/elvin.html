My journey into coding

I came to programming sideways, through design. For a long time I made posters for school events and small logos for people who paid me in snacks, and I genuinely believed that was as close to technology as I would ever get. Code looked like the opposite of what I enjoyed: rigid where design was flexible, unforgiving where design let you nudge things until they felt right.

What changed my mind was a website I designed and then watched somebody build badly. Every soft edge I had drawn came out sharp. Spacing I had agonised over turned into whatever the default was. I was frustrated enough to open an HTML file myself, and within about an hour I understood something that has shaped everything since: the browser is a design tool, and CSS is the part of design that actually ships.

The first six months were still rough. I learned layout by breaking it, learned accessibility by discovering that my favourite colour combination was unreadable, and learned JavaScript last and slowly, because I kept hoping I would not need it. I did need it. Learning those things in a sensible order would have saved me a season, which is precisely the problem this whole project exists to solve.

At Pung Academy I own the interface. That means the visual system, the components, and a long list of small decisions about what a beginner should see first, second, and not at all yet. I also argue for removing things. A roadmap that shows you fourteen options is not more helpful than one that shows you the next step — it is just louder.
